### 1. What was the most uncomfortable trade-off you made because of the time pressure? Be specific — name the trade-off, not the feeling.

The most uncomfortable trade-off was performing the heavy recalculation of audits synchronously inside the `POST /api/detect-changes` endpoint, rather than offloading it to a background worker queue. Given the strict time constraint, standing up robust worker infrastructure (like Upstash or Inngest) felt too risky. Instead, the detection endpoint pulls all non-stale audits into memory, runs the `aggregateAudit` engine against the current pricing for each one, and maps the diffs inline before sending grouped emails. While this works flawlessly for a small dataset, it is a significant architectural bottleneck. If 5,000 audits needed re-evaluation simultaneously, this single request would inevitably hit a Vercel function timeout or exhaust available memory. I actively traded fundamental system scalability and reliability for immediate feature completeness, knowing this specific architecture would collapse under real-world production load.

### 2. If we extended the deadline by another 24 hours right now, what's the first thing you'd do? (Not a wish list — the single first thing.)

The absolute first thing I would do is implement pagination and a queuing mechanism for the `detect-changes` endpoint to prevent the aforementioned timeouts. I would transition the endpoint from a synchronous processor to a lightweight job dispatcher. Specifically, the endpoint would only query the database for affected audit IDs in chunks, and then push those IDs into a robust queue like Vercel KV or Upstash QStash. A separate, dedicated worker endpoint would then process these batches asynchronously, run the audit engine to calculate the diffs, update the database, and dispatch the Resend emails safely. This single architectural shift would transition the feature from an MVP that crashes under moderate load to a resilient, production-ready system capable of handling tens of thousands of audits whenever a major vendor drops their pricing.

### 3. Looking back at your Round 1 codebase as a now-experienced user of it: what's one thing your Round 1 self made harder for your Round 2 self?

In Round 1, I made the rigid architectural decision to bundle the entire final audit result into a single `audit_data` JSONB column instead of normalizing the core data. At the time, it felt like a fast, clever way to avoid dealing with complex relational tables. However, in Round 2, this became a massive headache. Because the raw user inputs and the pricing snapshot were not explicitly preserved or separated from the final calculated output, the Re-audit feature forced me to awkwardly migrate the schema by adding new, overlapping columns (`input_stack`, `output_result`, `pricing_snapshot`) to compensate. Furthermore, my Round 1 UI was hardcoded to read directly from `audit_data`, meaning my Round 2 database updates silently crashed the public audit page until I manually wired up fallback logic. A normalized relational schema from day one would have made this iteration effortless.
